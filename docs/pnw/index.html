Forest Sight · Oregon & Washington

The Roadless Rule covers 3.98 million acres of forest in Oregon and Washington. The Forest Service has proposed ending it.

Since 2001, a national rule has barred new roads and most logging in the wildest parts of our national forests. In August 2026 the Forest Service proposed rescinding it. This page uses public data to show what is at stake in this region and what could change over the next 5 and 10 years.

What the rule does, and what is proposed

The 2001 Roadless Area Conservation Rule bars new road building and most timber harvest in "inventoried roadless areas" inside national forests. Nationwide that is about 58.5 million acres, and about 44 million acres where the national rule applies (Colorado and Idaho have their own state rules, so they are not covered here).

The proposal would remove the national rule. Decisions about roads and logging would then be made forest by forest, through each forest's management plan. Nothing would be built the day the rule ends. What changes is who decides, and what is allowed on paper.

The Forest Service's reasoning. USDA says more than 40% of roadless areas, mostly in the West, face high or very high wildfire hazard, and only 5% have had fuel-reduction treatment since 2014. It says the roadless restrictions make that risk worse.
The counter-argument. The Wilderness Society, a conservation group, analyzed Forest Service data from all eight contiguous-U.S. regions for 1992–2024 and reports that wildfires are four times more likely to start near roads than in roadless forests, because ignition density is much higher near roads. Its research appeared in the journal Fire Ecology. Critics also raise concerns about wildlife habitat, water, invasive species and cost. Source: The Wilderness Society.

Most roadless land is already close to a road

Roadless does not mean remote. Seen from an existing Forest Service road, a large share of this land is a short walk away. The Forest Service reports the same pattern nationally: 11.3 million of the 44 million acres are already near existing roads.

That closeness cuts both ways. New roads here would often be short extensions of roads that already exist. It also means this land is the easiest for a road to reach.

Nearly half of it overlaps habitat for threatened species

The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service designates "critical habitat" for species listed under the Endangered Species Act. We counted how much roadless land lies inside those boundaries.

This undercounts what is at stake. The habitat data used here does not include most fish streams, and it leaves out species that are not federally listed.

Invasive plants follow roads

Roads carry seeds on vehicles, equipment and road fill, and disturbed roadsides give invaders room to take hold. We tested whether that shows up in the data: how often are invasive plants recorded at each distance from a Forest Service road, compared with plant sightings in general?

Within about 330 feet of a road, invasive plants are recorded roughly 1.5 times more often than expected. Past about 800 feet the rate drops below normal, and between 0.6 and 1.2 miles it is about half of normal. This shows that roads and invasives go together. It does not by itself measure how fast new roads would spread them.

What could change in 5 and 10 years

Nobody can say how much would be built. So instead of one prediction, here are "what if" scenarios. Choose how far ahead to look.

Time ahead
How to read these. They are not forecasts. They show what the data implies if a given amount of land is opened. The Forest Service identifies about 4.8 million of the 44.7 million restricted acres (10.7%) where current plans allow active management. We assumed Oregon and Washington match that share, about 427,000 acres. The three scenarios assume that 5% / 10% / 20% of those acres see new roads or logging within 5 years, and 15% / 30% / 60% within 10 years. Those paces are our assumptions, not official figures.

Because this land is already close to roads, it already sits in the zone where invasive plants are most common, so opening it changes little: invasive pressure on the opened land stays about the same as today, rising to at most × today's level in the largest case. That is the favorable case for invasive plants. If activity instead reached more remote land, typically about 0.8 mile from a road, the same acreage could raise invasive pressure there to roughly × to × today's level. In either case about 40–45% of the opened acres would sit in critical habitat.

Invasive pressure is the typical rate at which invasives are recorded on the opened land, compared with its rate today, using the curve above. The low end assumes a quarter of each opened acre falls in a road's reach, the high end assumes all of it.

Methods and limits
  • Scope. Oregon and Washington national forests only. Colorado and Idaho have their own roadless rules and are excluded. Other regions will be added.
  • Data. Roadless areas, roads, forest boundaries and land ownership from the USDA Forest Service (FSGeodata). Critical habitat from the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service. Invasive-plant and general plant records from GBIF.
  • Observer bias. Plant records cluster near roads because that is where people look. We divide invasive records by all plant records at each distance to correct for most of that, but not all.
  • Roads and land. Only Forest Service roads were measured, and invasive-plant records are limited to land the Forest Service owns, so private land and towns inside forest boundaries are not counted. For that reason the Columbia River Gorge National Scenic Area, which has many highways and county roads, was left out of the invasive-plant analysis.
  • Scenarios. The eligible share is anchored to the Forest Service's national figure and the pace is our assumption. Treat results as ranges.
