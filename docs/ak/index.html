Forest Sight · Alaska

Alaska holds million acres of roadless forest, a third of everything the Roadless Rule covers.

Nearly all of it is in two national forests: the Tongass in Southeast Alaska and the Chugach around Prince William Sound. Alaska is unlike the Lower 48. It has almost no roads, no federally designated critical habitat in these forests, and a long legal fight over whether the rule applies at all. So this page tells Alaska's story with Alaska's own data: remoteness, salmon, old-growth forest and logging history.

Two forests, and the most contested roadless land in the country

The Tongass has been in and out of the national rule several times. It is covered today, and the proposal to rescind the rule would end that protection along with the rest.

Show the legal timeline for the Tongass
  • 2001. The Roadless Rule is issued and covers the Tongass.
  • 2003. The Bush administration agrees to exempt the Tongass from the rule.
  • 2011. A federal district court vacates the exemption, restoring protection to about 9.2 million acres. In 2014 a divided appeals panel reverses that, and in July 2015 an 11-judge Ninth Circuit panel rules the exemption illegal.
  • 2020. The Trump administration issues a final rule exempting the Tongass.
  • January 2023. The Forest Service reinstates roadless protection across about 9.2 million acres of the Tongass.
  • 2025–2026. The administration begins rescinding the rule. A proposed rule and environmental review are released on August 18, 2026.

Source: Earthjustice timeline of the Roadless Rule, a legal advocacy organization. See also Alaska Public Media.

Methods and limits
  • Data. Roadless areas, roads, land ownership, timber harvest records and the forest plan's old-growth and timber-suitability maps from the USDA Forest Service. Salmon streams from the Alaska Department of Fish and Game's Anadromous Waters Catalog.
  • Roads. Distances are to the nearest road in the Forest Service road data (about km in these forests). Roads on state, private and Alaska Native corporation land are not included, so some roadless land is closer to a road than shown.
  • No critical habitat. No federally designated critical habitat overlaps these roadless areas, so the Endangered Species Act lens used for the Lower 48 regions does not describe what is at stake here.
  • Logging history. Acres come from the polygon shapes in the Forest Service harvest records and are approximate. Records without a completion year are left out. A cut counts as "roadless" only if it falls in today's inventoried roadless areas.
  • Scenarios. They show past logging rates, not predictions, and not only logging inside roadless land. The Chugach is not modeled for logging: it has almost no logging history.
